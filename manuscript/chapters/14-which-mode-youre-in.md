# Fight 14: Which Mode You're In

*Gunatraya Vibhaga Yoga, "The Yoga of the Three Gunas" (Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 14)*

---

## Scene

You've started noticing you're basically three different people depending on the week.

There's the version of you that wakes up early, drinks water before coffee, actually enjoys the walk to class or work, feels clear-headed and unbothered by small annoyances. That version lasts maybe four days before it's gone.

Then there's the version that shows up after: wired, grindy, saying yes to everything, three tabs of work open at once, snapping at people over nothing, riding a kind of jittery momentum that feels like productivity but tastes like static. That version usually crashes.

And then there's the crash. The version that hasn't showered by 4 p.m., that's rewatched the same show for the fourth time instead of doing the one thing on the to-do list, that feels foggy and heavy and vaguely ashamed of how the day went, again. You've started calling this one "bed rotting," like everyone your age does, like it's a bit, but it doesn't feel like a bit from the inside.

What gets you is that none of these feel like choices. You don't wake up and pick which version shows up. You just find yourself already inside one of them, again, and spend the day being run by it instead of the other way around.

---

## Shloka

Chapter 14 names exactly what you've been noticing, with more precision than "good days and bad days."

> **BG 14.5** *(paraphrased)*
>
> Sattva, rajas, and tamas, these three qualities, born from nature, bind the eternal self to the body.

Sattva: clarity, lightness, calm, the early-morning-walk version. Rajas: passion, restlessness, craving, the wired-grindy-saying-yes-to-everything version. Tamas: inertia, heaviness, confusion, delusion, the bed-rotting version. Krishna describes each one's texture in detail:

> **BG 14.6–8** *(paraphrased)*
>
> Sattva, being pure, brings light and freedom from illness, but binds through attachment to happiness and knowledge. Rajas, full of passion, is born from craving and attachment, and binds through attachment to action. Tamas, born of ignorance, deludes all beings, binding through carelessness, laziness, and excess sleep.

All three bind. That's the part easy to miss if you've been treating sattva as "the good one to stay in forever." Even clarity and calm, clung to and identified with, becomes its own trap, a quieter one, but a trap. The goal was never to live permanently in the calm-morning-walk version and never leave. It was to stop being unconsciously cycled through all three like weather you have no say in.

> **BG 14.22–23** *(paraphrased)*
>
> One who is beyond the three gunas neither hates light, activity, or delusion when they arise, nor longs for them when they're absent. Such a person sits as a witness, undisturbed, knowing it is only the gunas in motion, and remains unshaken.

Not a fourth mood to chase. A different relationship to the three you already cycle through.

---

## Real Talk

The point was never to become a person who lives permanently in sattva and never has a rajas or tamas day again. That's not how the gunas work, and chasing that is its own rajasic craving in disguise. The actual shift this chapter is describing is smaller and more useful: noticing which mode you're in while you're in it, instead of being fully absorbed by it, thinking it's just who you are this week.

"I'm in a tamas cycle right now" is a completely different sentence than "I'm lazy and broken," even if the Tuesday looks identical from the outside. One of them passes, because weather passes. The other one sounds like a permanent verdict, and permanent verdicts are exactly the thing this whole book has been trying to talk you out of believing about yourself.

The wired, grindy rajas days aren't secretly the good days either, even though hustle culture will tell you otherwise. They burn out into a crash for a reason: they were never sustainable, they were just fast. And the calm sattva days aren't a permanent destination you failed to stay in. They're a mode, same as the other two, worth noticing and enjoying without gripping so tight that its inevitable departure feels like a personal failure.

You're not three different people. You're one person, cycling through three familiar weathers, slowly learning to watch the sky instead of becoming whatever cloud happens to be passing through it.

---

## Takeaway

**You're not lazy on the heavy days or virtuous on the clear ones. You're cycling through three real, nameable modes, the same three everyone cycles through.** The work isn't picking your favorite one and staying there forever. It's learning to notice which one you're in without becoming it completely.
