# Fight 3: No Neutral

*Karma Yoga, "The Yoga of Action" (Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 3)*

---

## Scene

You haven't applied anywhere in four months.

Not because there's nothing to apply for. Your laptop has three tabs open right now with half-finished forms in them. You just can't make yourself hit submit, because submitting means picking a direction, and picking a direction means all the other directions stop being available to you, and what if you pick wrong. So instead you keep the tabs open. You keep all your options technically alive. You tell people you're "still figuring it out," and it comes out sounding like patience instead of what it actually is, which is fear wearing patience as a disguise.

Your family keeps asking, gently at first and less gently now, what the plan is. You don't have one, because a plan requires closing doors, and you've built your whole personality lately around not closing doors. You've told yourself this is what being thoughtful looks like. Some nights you almost believe it.

Meanwhile the semester's ending. The internship deadline passed. Two of your friends already signed offers. You didn't do nothing, exactly, you scrolled job boards for hours most days, you just never once let it become a real decision. You've been so afraid of choosing wrong that you've quietly, accidentally, chosen nothing, and nothing is filling up your calendar faster than anything else would have.

---

## Shloka

Arjuna, still looking for an exit, asks Krishna something reasonable-sounding in the opening of this chapter: if knowledge is better than action, why push me toward action at all? Why not just let me withdraw completely (3.1–2)?

Krishna's answer cuts off the exit before Arjuna can even reach it.

> **BG 3.5** *(paraphrased)*
>
> No one, not for a single moment, actually exists without acting. Everyone is driven to act, constantly, by the very nature they're made of.

There's no neutral gear here. Even sitting still, even keeping every tab open and refusing to submit, is a choice your body and mind are actively making, over and over, all day. "I haven't decided yet" isn't a pause button on life. It's just a decision that doesn't want to admit it's a decision.

Krishna goes further: someone who sits motionless while their mind churns through everything they're avoiding is worse off than someone who just acts, imperfectly, in the world (3.6–7). Withdrawal without inner stillness isn't peace. It's just noise with nowhere to go.

Later in the chapter comes the line this book keeps circling back to, because every chapter needs it in a different shape:

> **BG 3.35** *(paraphrased)*
>
> Better to do your own path imperfectly than someone else's perfectly. Dying while doing what's actually yours to do is safer than succeeding at a life that was never yours.

And underneath all of it, Krishna names the actual mechanism jamming Arjuna up, the thing that turns a person into someone who can't choose: desire and anger, born from the same root, distorting judgment like smoke covers fire or dust covers a mirror (3.37–38). Not "wanting things" in general. The specific, gripping kind of wanting that makes every option feel like a threat if you don't pick it, and makes every choice you don't pick feel like a loss you'll never recover.

---

## Real Talk

Here's the part that stings a little: keeping your options open was never actually free. Every semester you don't apply anywhere is a semester spent, not saved. Every tab you leave half-filled is a version of you that already decided, quietly, to stay exactly where you are. You've been treating indecision like it doesn't cost anything, like it's a waiting room outside of time, and it isn't. It has been costing you the whole time, you just weren't calling it a cost.

This isn't really about ambition or productivity. It's about what fear does when you let it drive long enough. Somewhere in there, "I don't want to pick wrong" quietly turned into "I won't pick at all," and that felt safer, right up until you noticed everyone around you was somewhere and you were still in the parking lot with your engine running.

Krishna's answer isn't "pick the perfect thing." It's closer to: pick your thing, the one that's actually yours, even imperfectly, and go do it, because standing still was never actually the safe option. It only felt that way. Doing your own thing badly beats performing someone else's life well, and it definitely beats doing nobody's life at all.

---

## Takeaway

**There's no neutral. Staying still is still a choice, and it's usually the most expensive one on the table.** Pick the thing that's actually yours, even if you do it badly at first. Standing in the parking lot with the engine running was never as safe as it felt.
